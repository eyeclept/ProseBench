## Rule 4: Only standard US-keyboard characters

Every character in ordinary prose must be one a standard US keyboard can type directly. This bans em dashes, en dashes, curly/smart quotes, and ellipsis glyphs: none of them sit on a US keyboard, and each has a plain ASCII substitute. A foreign word or proper name that is only correctly spelled with a diacritic or non-Latin letter (café, naïve, Motörhead, Zoë) keeps its real spelling, that's not this violation, it's spelling the word right. Reaching for a typographic character in place of its ASCII equivalent in ordinary prose always is.

The em dash is the most common offender, and the one where the fix takes judgment. Use a semicolon, a period, a comma, or restructure. Choosing among
those is not mechanical: an em dash usually joins two independent clauses, and dropping in a
comma without checking clause structure produces a run-on -- specifically a comma splice,
two independent clauses joined by only a comma. Two independent clauses take a semicolon or
a period; a trailing fragment (not its own clause) takes a comma; a sentence that would run
on either way gets split into two sentences instead.

- WRONG: "The policy -- which affected millions -- was later reversed."
- RIGHT: "The policy affected millions of devices. The company reversed it in December 2017."
- WRONG (comma splice -- two independent clauses joined by a bare comma): "The device
  shipped in 2019, it failed within a year."
- RIGHT: "The device shipped in 2019; it failed within a year."

The rest are a plain character swap, no judgment call needed: an en dash used as a hyphen or a range separator becomes a hyphen ("1990-2000", not an en dash); a curly/smart quotation mark (the left/right single and double marks a word processor or AI tool inserts automatically) becomes a straight apostrophe (') or straight double quote ("); a single ellipsis glyph becomes three separate period characters ("...").
